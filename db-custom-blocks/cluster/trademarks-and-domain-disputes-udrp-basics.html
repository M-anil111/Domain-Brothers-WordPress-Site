A domain name can be perfectly valid and still cause a legal problem if it clashes with a trademark. Most domain purchases are free of disputes, and a few simple checks keep it that way. This article explains how trademarks and domains interact and what the main dispute process, the UDRP, involves. It is part of our complete guide to premium domain names. This is general information and not legal advice. For a specific situation, speak to a trademark lawyer.

In short

  • Owning a domain does not give you trademark rights, and a trademark can override a domain.
  • Check trademarks in the markets you serve before you buy.
  • The UDRP is a fast process in which a trademark owner can ask for a domain to be transferred or cancelled.
  • A name that is a common word or phrase, used in good faith for its ordinary meaning, is usually safer.

How trademarks and domains relate

A trademark protects a name, logo or phrase used to identify goods or services. A domain name is a registered address. They are separate systems, and they can collide. If a domain is identical or confusingly similar to someone else's trademark and is used to trade on it, the trademark owner may have a claim.

Context matters. A common word can be a trademark in one field and an ordinary word in another. A domain made of the ordinary word, used for the ordinary meaning, is generally in a stronger position than a domain that copies a brand name.

Check a name before you buy

  1. Search the trademark registers for your markets. In the United States, use the USPTO trademark search; other countries have their own registers, and some have regional ones.
  2. Search the web for the name and look at who uses it and for what.
  3. Check similar spellings and phrases, not only the exact name.
  4. Look at the domain's history on a web archive.
  5. Ask the seller whether they have received any complaints about the name.
  6. Consider the field. The same word can be fine for a bakery and risky for a software company if a well-known software brand owns it.

For purchases that matter, get a legal opinion and keep it with your records.

What the UDRP is

The Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy was adopted by ICANN and is built into the registration contract for most generic domains. You can read the policy on ICANN's UDRP page, and the Wikipedia summary of the UDRP is a readable overview. Providers such as the WIPO Arbitration and Mediation Center run the cases, and WIPO publishes a guide to the process.

The three things a complainant must show

  1. The domain is identical or confusingly similar to a trademark in which the complainant has rights.
  2. The domain holder has no rights or legitimate interests in the name.
  3. The domain was registered and is being used in bad faith.

All three are needed. If the complainant wins, the usual remedy is transfer or cancellation of the domain. Money damages are not part of the process.

How a case works

  • The complainant files a written complaint with a provider.
  • The domain holder gets a chance to respond in writing.
  • A panel of one or three decides on the documents, usually within about two months.
  • The registrar carries out the decision unless the holder starts a court case.

How to protect yourself as a buyer

  • Buy names made of ordinary words for their ordinary meaning.
  • Avoid names that copy a brand, even with a small change.
  • Keep records of why you chose the name, and of the date and price.
  • Use the name in good faith for a real business or project.
  • Respond to complaints promptly and take advice. Ignoring a notice is the quickest way to lose a name.

If you receive a complaint

  1. Do not panic and do not ignore it.
  2. Read the notice and note the deadlines.
  3. Contact a lawyer who knows domain disputes.
  4. Collect your evidence: when and why you registered the name, how you use it, and any correspondence.
  5. Consider whether a settlement makes sense. Many cases end by agreement.

If someone is using your trademark in a domain

Gather evidence of your rights and of the domain's use, send a polite notice if appropriate, and consider the UDRP or a court case. A lawyer can tell you which path is cheaper and faster for your situation.
