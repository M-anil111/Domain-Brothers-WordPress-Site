A domain name can be perfectly valid and still end up in a UDRP case if it clashes with a trademark. Most domain purchases are free of disputes, and a few simple checks keep it that way. This article explains how trademarks and domains interact and what the main dispute process, the UDRP, involves. It is part of our complete guide to premium domain names. This is general information and not legal advice. For a specific situation, speak to a trademark lawyer.

In short

  • Owning a domain does not give you trademark rights, and a trademark can override a domain.
  • Check trademarks in the markets you serve before you buy.
  • The UDRP is a fast process in which a trademark owner can ask for a domain to be transferred or cancelled.
  • A name that is a common word or phrase, used in good faith for its ordinary meaning, is usually safer.

How trademarks and domains relate

A trademark protects a name, logo or phrase used to identify goods or services. A domain name is a registered address. They are separate systems, and they can collide. If a domain is identical or confusingly similar to someone else's trademark and is used to trade on it, the trademark owner may have a claim.

Context matters. A common word can be a trademark in one field and an ordinary word in another. A domain made of the ordinary word, used for the ordinary meaning, is generally in a stronger position than a domain that copies a brand name.

Check a name before you buy

  1. Search the trademark registers for your markets. In the United States, use the USPTO trademark search; other countries have their own registers, and some have regional ones.
  2. Search the web for the name and look at who uses it and for what.
  3. Check similar spellings and phrases, not only the exact name.
  4. Look at the domain's history on a web archive.
  5. Ask the seller whether they have received any complaints about the name.
  6. Consider the field. The same word can be fine for a bakery and risky for a software company if a well-known software brand owns it.

For purchases that matter, get a legal opinion and keep it with your records.

What the UDRP is

The Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy was adopted by ICANN and is built into the registration contract for most generic domains. You can read the policy on ICANN's UDRP page, and the Wikipedia summary of the UDRP is a readable overview. Providers such as the WIPO Arbitration and Mediation Center run the cases, and WIPO publishes a guide to the process.

The three things a complainant must show

  1. The domain is identical or confusingly similar to a trademark in which the complainant has rights.
  2. The domain holder has no rights or legitimate interests in the name.
  3. The domain was registered and is being used in bad faith.

All three are needed. If the complainant wins, the usual remedy is transfer or cancellation of the domain. Money damages are not part of the process.

The three UDRP elements in more detail

Each element has its own tests, and WIPO publishes an overview of how panels apply them.

Element 1: identical or confusingly similar

Panels compare the domain with the trademark side by side and usually ignore the extension. A domain that contains the whole mark, even with an added word, is often found confusingly similar. This is a low bar; most cases turn on the other two elements.

Element 2: no rights or legitimate interests

The UDRP lists ways a domain holder can show a legitimate interest:

  • Using the name, or preparing to use it, for a genuine offering of goods or services before any notice of the dispute.
  • Being commonly known by the name, as a person, business or organisation.
  • Making a legitimate non-commercial or fair use of the name, without intent to mislead consumers or tarnish the mark.

A dictionary word used for its dictionary meaning is a common legitimate interest.

Element 3: bad faith under the UDRP

The policy's examples include registering a name mainly to sell it to the trademark owner or a competitor for more than the registration costs, blocking owners from using their marks as a pattern, disrupting a competitor, and attracting visitors for profit by creating confusion with the mark. A domain registered years before the trademark existed is rarely found to be registered in bad faith.

How a case works

  • The complainant files a written complaint with a provider.
  • The domain holder gets a chance to respond in writing.
  • A panel of one or three decides on the documents, usually within about two months.
  • The registrar carries out the decision unless the holder starts a court case.

The UDRP timeline, step by step

Short deadlines make the process faster than a lawsuit. A typical case under the WIPO process runs like this:

  1. Complaint filed. The complainant files and pays the provider's fee.
  2. Domain locked. The registrar confirms the holder and locks the domain while the case runs.
  3. Case starts. The provider notifies the domain holder.
  4. Response due. The holder has 20 days to file a written response with evidence.
  5. Panel appointed. The provider appoints a panel of one or three members.
  6. Decision. The panel normally decides within 14 days of its appointment, on the written record. There are no hearings.
  7. Implementation. The registrar waits 10 business days before a transfer or cancellation, and holds off if the holder starts a court case in that time.

Who pays for a UDRP case

The complainant pays the provider's published fee. A holder pays nothing to respond unless it asks for a three-member panel, in which case the two sides share that cost. Each side pays its own lawyer.

Possible UDRP outcomes

A panel can reach one of three results:

  • Transfer: the domain moves to the complainant. This is the most common outcome when a complaint succeeds.
  • Cancellation: the registration is deleted. Complainants rarely ask for this, because the name can then be registered by someone else.
  • Complaint denied: the holder keeps the domain.

Decisions are published. There is no appeal within the process, but either side can go to court.

Reverse domain name hijacking

If a complainant brought a case in bad faith, for example to pressure an owner into giving up a name it could not buy, the panel can declare reverse domain name hijacking. The finding carries no fine.

The UDRP and other dispute processes

The policy covers .com, .net, .org and other generic extensions, but it is not the only route.

  • Uniform Rapid Suspension (URS): a faster process for newer generic extensions and the clearest cases. The only remedy is suspension.
  • Country-code policies: many country-code extensions run their own process. For example, .uk disputes go to Nominet's dispute resolution service.
  • Courts: either side can go to court at any stage. In the United States, the Anticybersquatting Consumer Protection Act can award damages.

How to protect yourself as a buyer

  • Buy names made of ordinary words for their ordinary meaning.
  • Avoid names that copy a brand, even with a small change.
  • Keep records of why you chose the name, and of the date and price.
  • Use the name in good faith for a real business or project.
  • Respond to complaints promptly and take advice. Ignoring a notice is the quickest way to lose a name.

If you receive a complaint

  1. Do not panic and do not ignore it.
  2. Read the notice and note the deadlines.
  3. Contact a lawyer who knows domain disputes.
  4. Collect your evidence: when and why you registered the name, how you use it, and any correspondence.
  5. Consider whether a settlement makes sense. Many cases end by agreement.

If someone is using your trademark in a domain

Gather evidence of your rights and of the domain's use, send a polite notice if appropriate, and consider the UDRP or a court case. A lawyer can tell you which path is cheaper and faster for your situation.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a UDRP case take?

Most UDRP cases take about two months from filing to decision. The holder has 20 days to respond, and the panel normally decides within 14 days of its appointment. If the panel orders a transfer, the registrar waits 10 business days before acting, to allow the holder to start a court case.

Can I lose a domain under the UDRP if I bought it in good faith?

The complainant has to prove all three elements, including bad faith at registration and in use. A name made of common words, bought and used for their ordinary meaning, is in a strong position. Keep records that show why and when you chose the name, and respond to any complaint on time.

What happens if I ignore a UDRP complaint?

The panel decides on the complainant's evidence alone. Panels still check that all three elements are met, but without a response the holder loses the chance to show a legitimate interest or good faith. Ignoring a complaint is the quickest way to lose a name.
