Short domain names are the scarcest names on the internet, because the number of possible combinations is small and fixed. A two, three or four character name is easy to type, fits on any screen or sign and works in every language. This guide explains how many short names exist, how the market groups them and what decides their price. It is part of our guide on how to buy a domain name for a website.

In short

  • Letters alone give 676 two letter, 17,576 three letter and 456,976 four letter combinations in each extension.
  • Every two and three letter .com is registered, so buying one means buying from an existing owner.
  • Pronounceable names and real acronyms usually sell for more than random letter strings of the same length.
  • Price depends on length, extension, the letters used, pronounceability and comparable sales.

Why short domain names are scarce

The supply of short domain names is a simple calculation. With 26 letters, each position in the name has 26 options, so the number of combinations is 26 raised to the length of the name. Add the ten digits and each position has 36 options.

Those totals apply to each extension separately. There are only 676 possible two letter .com names, and no registry can create more. Hyphens add a few extra combinations, but buyers rarely want them in a name this short.

Compare that with the hundreds of millions of registered domains worldwide and the scarcity is clear. Every two and three letter .com has been registered for years, and almost all four letter .coms are registered too. Short domain names in .com therefore trade on the aftermarket, from one owner to the next.

Two letter domains

Two letter domains are the rarest group. With only 676 letter combinations per extension, many are held by large companies, long-term investors or the original registrants from the early years of the web. Some are used as the main address of a global brand; others serve as short links or email domains.

Two letter names are also used as country codes at the top level, which is a separate system. A two letter name inside .com is a second-level name owned like any other domain. The Wikipedia article on single-letter second-level domains explains why the very shortest names are rarer still, since many registries reserved them.

When two letter domains in .com change hands, the sale is usually private and slow. Expect a broker on both sides and a careful check of ownership history.

Three letter domains

Three letter domains are often called LLL names in the trade, where L stands for letter. Their appeal comes from acronyms. Thousands of companies, associations and products use three-letter initials, so many LLL names have several possible buyers.

Within the 17,576 combinations, value varies widely:

  • Common acronyms that match many company or product names usually attract the most interest.
  • Names that read as a word or syllable are easier to say and remember.
  • Strings of awkward consonants are harder to sell to an end user, though they still hold value because of scarcity.
  • Letter choice matters. Investors often treat names without vowels and certain letters as a separate class, because of demand from particular markets.

4 letter domains: pronounceable vs random

With 456,976 combinations, 4 letter domains are far more plentiful than three letter names, so the gap between strong and weak names is wider. Two names of the same length can differ in price by a large margin purely because of how they sound.

Among short domain names, random strings such as four consonants in a row usually suit acronyms and investors. Pronounceable four letter names, by contrast, can work as a full brand on their own. Browse our four character domains to see both kinds side by side.

Pronounceable short domain names

A pronounceable name follows a speakable pattern, for example consonant-vowel-consonant-vowel, or a real short word. These short domain names pass the radio test: hear it once, type it correctly. That makes them useful as brands, not just as acronyms, and our guide to brandable domain names explains how buyers judge them.

Real dictionary words of four letters are rarer than invented pronounceable names and are priced accordingly. Invented ones give you a distinct brand that is easier to protect as a trademark.

Who buys short domain names and why

Short domain names attract three main groups of buyers, and each values them for a different reason. Knowing which group you belong to helps you decide how short you really need to go.

  • Companies with matching initials. A business, association or product known by its initials often wants the exact acronym. For these buyers, the name protects the brand and stops email or visitors going elsewhere.
  • Brands that want a one-word identity. A pronounceable four or five letter name can become the brand itself. These buyers care most about sound, spelling and trademark fit.
  • Investors. Because the supply is fixed, many short names are held as long-term assets. Investors watch comparable sales closely and price their names against them.

Short domain names also suit practical uses beyond the main website: link shorteners, email addresses that staff must type often, QR codes on packaging, and campaign addresses printed on small surfaces. If your use is one of these, a short name in a less common extension may serve you well at a lower price.

Before you buy, ask what the short name must do. If people will mostly click links, length matters less. If they will hear the name and type it, every character counts.

What sets the price of short domain names

No two short domain names are priced the same way, but the same factors move the number up or down.

  1. Length. Each character removed shrinks the supply sharply, as the table above shows.
  2. Extension. A .com name is usually worth more than the same letters in another extension. Country codes can be strong in their own market.
  3. Letters used. Common initials and letters that suit many languages raise demand. Rarely used letters lower it.
  4. Pronounceability. A name you can say as a word appeals to more end users than a string you must spell out.
  5. Meaning. An acronym used by a known industry body, or a short real word, adds buyers.
  6. Comparable sales. Recorded sales of similar names are the best evidence of value. Our guide on how to value a premium domain name shows how to use them.
  7. History. A clean record, with no spam or trademark disputes, protects the price.

Prices for short domain names are set by owners, not registries. The Wikipedia overview of domain name speculation gives background on how investors came to hold so many of them.

Five character and numeric names

If two to four letters are out of reach, two nearby groups offer much of the same benefit. Five character names are still short enough to type quickly and often include real words or clean brandable forms; see our five character domains.

Frequently asked questions

How many three letter .com domains are there?

There are 17,576 possible three letter .com names using letters only, because each of the three positions can hold any of 26 letters: 26 x 26 x 26. All of them are registered. If you include digits, the total rises to 46,656, since each position then has 36 possible characters.

Are any two letter .com domains still available to register?

No. All 676 letter-only two letter .com names are registered, and most have been for many years. To get one you must buy it from its current owner, usually through a broker. The same is true of every three letter .com, so these names are bought on the aftermarket rather than registered new.

Why do some 4 letter domains cost far more than others?

Pronounceability, meaning and letter choice. A four letter name that reads as a word or a real brand can serve many businesses, so more buyers compete for it. A random string mostly appeals to companies with matching initials. Extension, comparable sales and the name's history also move the price.

Is a short domain always better than a longer one?

Not always. A short name is easier to type and remember, but a slightly longer name that clearly says what you do can serve a business better and cost far less. Judge the name on how customers will hear, spell and recall it, not on character count alone, and compare the total cost.
