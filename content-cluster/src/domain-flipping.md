Domain flipping is the practice of buying domain names at a low price and reselling them for more. The mechanics are simple, but the economics are harder than most guides admit: most names never sell, and each one costs a renewal fee every year. End users are the buyers investors need, so read this alongside our guide on [how to buy a domain name for a website](/how-to-buy-a-domain-name-for-a-website/).

!takeaways In short
- Domain flipping is a slow, uncertain business; there are no reliable income figures and no guaranteed returns.
- Renewal fees on unsold names are the main ongoing cost, and they add up across a portfolio.
- Good investors buy names an end user would want, not names that only other investors like.
- Trademark conflicts can cost you the name and more, so screen every purchase.

## What is domain flipping?

!img:ib-flipping-a|The domain flipping cycle: Research, Buy, Hold, List, Sell|The domain flipping cycle.

Domain flipping is a short-cycle form of domain investing. An investor registers or buys a name, holds it, and sells it to someone who values it more, usually a business that wants it for a website. The "flip" is the resale.

Some investors aim for a quick sale to other investors at a small margin. Others hold names for years waiting for the right end user. The [Wikipedia article on domain name speculation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_name_speculation) traces how this market developed since the early days of the commercial web.

### Domain flipping vs long-term domain name investing

The difference is mostly time horizon. Domain flipping focuses on turning names over quickly. Long-term domain name investing accepts that a strong name may take years to find its buyer, and prices for that patience. Both depend on the same skill: judging which names someone else will pay for.

## The real costs and risks of domain flipping

Before you buy a single name, understand where the money goes. A typical portfolio has these costs:

- **Acquisition.** New registrations are cheap; aftermarket names and expired domain auctions are not.
- **Renewals.** Every name renews every year whether it sells or not. This is the cost that sinks most beginners.
- **Selling costs.** Marketplaces and brokers take a commission on each sale.
- **Your time.** Research, listing, answering enquiries and negotiating all take hours.

The core problem is that sales are infrequent and unpredictable. You pay renewals on the whole portfolio while only a small share of names sell in any given year. A portfolio can lose money for years even if a few names eventually sell well.

| Cost or risk | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Renewal fees | Recur every year on every unsold name |
| Low sell-through | Most names may never sell, so winners must cover the losers |
| Commission | Reduces the amount you keep from each sale |
| Trademark claims | Can lead to losing the name and paying legal costs |
| Price changes | Registries can raise renewal prices on some extensions |

Nobody can promise that domain flipping will produce income. Treat any course, tool or seller that does so with suspicion.

## How investors pick names to resell

Good picks share one trait: an obvious end user who would want the name for a real business. Names that only other investors find clever rarely sell for much.

Investors who resell domains successfully tend to look for a few traits.

They ask who would build a business on the name, and they want a clear answer before they buy. In practice that means:

- **Short, clear names** in .com or a strong country code for its own market.
- **Real words and natural phrases** that describe a product, service or industry.
- **Brandable names** that are easy to say and spell. Our guide to [brandable domain names](/brandable-domain-names/) explains what makes one work.
- **Clean history,** checked in a web archive and against trademark databases.
- **Comparable sales** that show similar names have sold before.

Avoid typos of famous brands, names built around a company's trademark and names that only make sense during a passing trend. These are the riskiest purchases in domain investing.

### Where domain flipping investors find names

Common sources include new registrations of unclaimed names, expired domain auctions and drop catching services, and private purchases from other owners. ## Trademark risk in domain flipping

This is the risk that can turn a cheap name into an expensive mistake. If a name matches or imitates a trademark, the brand owner can file a complaint under the Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy (UDRP) and may win the name. In some countries, they can also sue.

Panels look at whether the name is confusingly similar to a mark, whether the owner has a legitimate interest, and whether the name was registered and used in bad faith. Offering a name for sale to the brand owner is a classic sign of bad faith. [WIPO's domain name dispute resources](https://www.wipo.int/amc/en/domains/) publish the decisions and the process.

Practical rules for anyone doing domain flipping:

- Search trademark databases for every name before you buy it.
- Do not buy names that only have value because of someone else's brand.
- Never approach a trademark owner offering to sell a name that matches their mark.

Our article on [trademarks and domain disputes](/trademarks-and-domain-disputes-udrp-basics/) covers the UDRP in more detail.

## How to price names you want to resell

Pricing is where domain flipping turns into either sales or a long wait. A good price is one an end user can accept without a long debate, while still covering your costs across the portfolio.

1. **Start from comparable sales,** not from what you paid or from listed prices.
2. **Consider the buyer.** A business that would build its brand on the name will pay more than another investor.
3. **Factor in holding costs.** The longer you expect to wait, the more renewals the price must cover.
4. **Decide fixed price or offers.** A visible price often sells faster; an offer form suits rare names.

Our guide on [how to value a premium domain name](/how-to-value-a-premium-domain-name/) gives a repeatable valuation method that works for sellers too.

### Handling offers and negotiation

Many enquiries open with a low offer. Reply politely, give a counter-offer, and explain it briefly with facts such as length, extension or comparable sales. Buyers respond better to reasons than to round numbers.

Decide your floor price before the conversation starts, and do not reveal it. If a buyer cannot reach your floor, a courteous "not at this price" keeps the door open. Some buyers return months later with a better offer, especially once their business has grown.

## How to list and sell your names

Once a name is priced, it needs exposure. Investors who resell domains usually combine several channels:

- **Marketplace listings** with a buy-now price or an offer form.
- **A "for sale" landing page** on the domain itself, so direct visitors can enquire.
- **Brokers** for higher-value names.
- **Direct outreach** to businesses using similar names, kept short and factual.

When a buyer agrees, protect yourself. Use escrow or a trusted marketplace checkout, and release the transfer code only after payment has cleared. Our guide on [how to sell a domain name](/sell-my-domain/) walks through payment and transfer step by step.

## Common beginner mistakes

Most early losses in domain investing come from a short list of avoidable errors.

- **Buying too many names too fast.** A large portfolio of weak names multiplies renewal costs without adding many sales.
- **Falling for clever names.** Puns, invented spellings and names tied to a single news story rarely find a business buyer.
- **Ignoring the extension.** A good word on an extension few people use is a much harder sale.
- **Pricing by hope.** Asking prices far above comparable sales usually mean no enquiries at all.
- **Skipping the trademark check.** One complaint can cost more than the profit from several sales.
- **Not tracking numbers.** Without a record of costs and sales, you cannot tell whether the portfolio works.

### Keep records from the first name

Track each name's purchase price, purchase date, renewal date, renewal cost, where it is listed and every offer you receive. A simple spreadsheet is enough. Rules on how domain sales are taxed differ between countries, so ask an accountant how your sales should be reported before you start.

Review the portfolio once a year, before the main renewal dates. Drop names that have had no interest and no clear end user. Letting weak names expire is part of the discipline, not a failure.

## Is domain flipping right for you?

Domain flipping can suit people who enjoy research, accept uncertainty and can afford to lose what they put in. It does not suit anyone who needs a dependable monthly income. Start small, track every cost, and judge your results over several years rather than months.
