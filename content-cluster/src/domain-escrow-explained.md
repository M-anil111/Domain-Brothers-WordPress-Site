Domain escrow is a way to buy or sell a domain name between two parties who do not know each other, with a neutral third party holding the money until the transfer is complete. It removes the biggest risk in a private sale: one side paying or handing over the name and the other side disappearing. This guide explains how it works step by step, what it costs, how criminals copy escrow sites, and what the alternatives are. It is part of our guide on [how to buy a domain name for a website](/how-to-buy-a-domain-name-for-a-website/).

- In domain escrow a licensed third party holds the buyer's payment, the seller transfers the name, and the money is released once the buyer confirms receipt.
- Fees are usually a percentage of the price, sometimes with a minimum, and the parties agree who pays them.
- Fake escrow sites are a common scam: always type the provider's address yourself and check its licence.

## What is domain escrow?

!img:ib-escrow-a|How domain escrow works: Agree, Pay, Transfer, Confirm, Release|How domain escrow works.

[Escrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escrow) is an old arrangement in which a neutral party holds money or property until agreed conditions are met. Domain escrow applies that idea to domain names. The escrow agent does not own the name or set the price. It holds the buyer's funds and releases them when both sides have done what they agreed.

A **domain escrow service** is useful when the buyer and seller are strangers, when the price is high enough that losing it would hurt, or when one side is in another country. It replaces trust in the other party with trust in a regulated intermediary.

## How domain escrow works, step by step

Most providers follow the same basic sequence, although the screens and names vary.

1. **Agree terms.** Buyer and seller agree the price, who pays the fee, the inspection period and how the name will move.
2. **Open the transaction.** One side starts the transaction on the provider's site and the other side accepts the same terms.
3. **Buyer pays the escrow agent.** The buyer sends funds to the provider, not to the seller.
4. **Agent confirms the funds.** The provider tells both sides that payment is secured.
5. **Seller transfers the name.** The seller moves the domain to the buyer, either by a push inside the same registrar or by giving the transfer (EPP) code for a move to another registrar.
6. **Buyer confirms receipt.** The buyer checks that the name is in their account and that they control it, then confirms.
7. **Agent releases payment.** The provider pays the seller, less any fees.

### Domain escrow and the transfer itself

The transfer step is where most delays happen. Registrar transfers can take several days, and some names cannot move to a new registrar for a period after certain events, such as a recent transfer. Agree in advance how long the seller has to start the transfer, and how long the buyer has to confirm. ## Choosing a licensed domain escrow service

Not every site that uses the word escrow is a real escrow company. The protection only works if the provider is properly licensed and actually holds funds separately from its own money.

Check these points before you use any provider:

- **Licence.** In the United States, escrow companies are generally licensed under state law. A reputable provider names its licence and regulator on its site, and you can confirm it with that regulator.
- **Track record.** Look for a provider that has handled domain sales for years and is widely used in the industry.
- **Domain experience.** The agent should understand registrar pushes, transfer codes and the checks needed to confirm control of a name.
- **Clear terms.** Fees, inspection periods and dispute steps should be written down before you pay.
- **Contact details.** A real company publishes how to reach it and answers questions before you commit.

If a provider cannot show a licence you can verify, do not use it, however professional the site looks.

## What does domain escrow cost?

Escrow for domain purchase transactions is usually priced as a percentage of the sale price, often with a minimum fee for small deals. The rate tends to fall as the price rises. The payment method can change the total: card payments and some international transfers cost more than a bank wire.

| Cost item | How it usually works |
|---|---|
| Escrow fee | A percentage of the price, often with a minimum |
| Who pays | Buyer, seller or split, as agreed before the deal opens |
| Payment method charges | Can differ between bank wire, card and other methods |
| Currency conversion | May apply when buyer and seller use different currencies |
| Optional extras | Some providers charge for added services, such as longer inspection periods |

Check the provider's own fee calculator for exact figures before you agree terms, and write down who pays what. A disagreement about fees halfway through a deal is avoidable.

## Fake escrow sites and other scams

Because escrow is trusted, criminals copy it. The most common trick is a fake escrow site built to look like a real provider. A form of [phishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phishing), it collects the buyer's money and then disappears.

### Warning signs of a fake domain escrow site

- The other party insists on a provider you have never heard of.
- They send you a link to the escrow site instead of letting you find it yourself.
- The site's address is slightly different from a known provider, with an extra word, letter or different extension.
- The site was registered recently, or has no licence you can check.
- You are asked to pay by gift card, cryptocurrency or a personal account.
- Emails claim funds are secured, but nothing appears when you log in directly.

### How to protect yourself

- Choose the provider yourself, and type its address into your browser.
- Log in directly to check every status update, rather than trusting emails.
- Confirm the licence with the regulator named on the site.
- Never send a transfer code or push a name until the escrow agent, through its own site, confirms that funds are secured.
- Keep every message and agreement in writing.

## Domain escrow from the seller's side

Escrow protects sellers too. Without it, a seller who transfers a name first can be left with nothing, or with a card payment that the buyer later disputes. With escrow, the seller can see that funds are secured before the name moves.

Sellers should take a few extra steps:

- Wait for the provider's own site to show that funds are secured, not an email or a screenshot from the buyer.
- Unlock the name and request the transfer code only once that confirmation appears.
- Check that the buyer details in the escrow transaction match the account receiving the name.
- Keep the domain renewed until the transfer completes, so it cannot lapse mid-deal.

Fake escrow sites target sellers as well, with emails claiming that payment has been made. Log in directly to check.

## Alternatives to domain escrow

Domain escrow is not the only safe route. The right one depends on who you are buying from.

- **Buy from a marketplace that holds the name.** When the seller already holds the domain and takes payment through a known payment processor, the risk of a stranger disappearing is lower.
- **Use a broker.** A broker who knows both sides can manage the deal and set out terms in writing before money moves. Our [domain brokerage](/domain-brokerage-services/) team works this way for names owned by others.
- **Registrar push between known parties.** When both sides use the same registrar and trust each other, a direct push can be simple, but someone still has to go first.
- **Paying a stranger directly.** This is the riskiest choice and is best avoided for any meaningful amount.
