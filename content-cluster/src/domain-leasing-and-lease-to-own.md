Domain leasing lets you use a domain name that someone else owns, in return for regular payments, instead of buying it outright. It can put a strong name within reach of a young business, but the owner keeps the name, and that changes the risk. This guide explains plain leases, lease to own deals, what happens when a payment is missed, and the contract terms worth checking. It is part of our guide on [how to buy a domain name for a website](/how-to-buy-a-domain-name-for-a-website/).

!takeaways In short
- In domain leasing the owner keeps the registration; you rent the right to point the name at your site and email.
- A lease to own domain deal adds a path to ownership after the last payment, but you usually own nothing until then.
- Read the default clause first: a missed payment can end your use of the name and keep the money you paid.
- A seller payment plan is a different route to the same goal; compare the terms side by side before you sign anything.

## What is domain leasing?

!img:ib-leasing-a|Domain leasing. Leasing, lease to own and payment plans: Lease, Lease to own, Payment plan|Leasing, lease to own and payment plans.

Domain leasing is a rental agreement for a domain name. The owner, often called the lessor, keeps the name registered in their own account. You, the lessee, pay a fee each month or year and in return the name points at your website or email.

People also call it **domain rental**, or say they **rent a domain**. The idea is close to renting an office: you get the use, the landlord keeps the title. A lease to own deal works much like a [rent-to-own](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rent-to-own) agreement for goods. When the lease ends, the name goes back to the owner unless you renew or buy it.

Owners lease names because it turns an idle asset into steady income and keeps the option to sell later. Lessees lease because the upfront cost is lower than buying, and because a better name can start working for them now.

## Plain leases and lease to own deals

There are two main forms, and the difference matters more than the monthly figure.

| Feature | Plain lease | Lease to own |
|---|---|---|
| Who holds the name during the term | The owner | The owner, or a platform acting for them |
| What you get at the end | Nothing, unless you renew | Ownership, once every payment is made |
| Typical length | Monthly or yearly, renewable | A fixed number of months |
| If you stop paying | Use of the name ends | Use ends, and payments are usually kept |
| Best for | Testing a name or a short campaign | A business that wants the name for good |

### How a lease to own domain deal works

A **lease to own domain** agreement fixes a total price and splits it into instalments. You use the name while you pay. When the last instalment clears, the owner transfers the registration to your registrar account and the name becomes yours.

Until that last payment, the name normally stays in the owner's account, or with a platform that holds it on their behalf. That protects the owner if you stop paying. It also means you depend on the owner, or the platform, keeping the name renewed and the settings correct for the whole term.

## Who holds the name during domain leasing

The registrant of record controls the name. In domain leasing that is the owner, not you. Whoever controls the registrar account can change the DNS settings, renew or let the name lapse, and move it to another registrar.

Most arrangements give the lessee control of the DNS records, or a way to request changes, so you can point the name at your host and set up email. Ask exactly how this works before you sign. You want to know:

- Whether you set the DNS records yourself or ask the owner each time.
- How fast changes are made, and who to contact if the site goes down.
- Who pays the annual registry renewal fee, and what proof you get that it is paid.
- Whether the owner can sell the name to someone else during the term.

Registrar transfers follow ICANN's [transfer policy](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/transfer-policy-2016-06-01-en), which allows a registrar to lock a name against a move to another registrar for a period after certain events, such as a recent transfer. Ask the owner's registrar which locks apply, and plan the final handover with that in mind.

## What happens if you miss a payment

This is the most important clause in any lease, and the one people read last. In most domain leasing contracts a missed payment starts a short grace period. If the payment is not made by the end of it, the lease ends.

When a lease ends for non-payment, the usual results are:

1. The owner points the name away from your site, often to a for-sale page.
2. Your email on that name stops working.
3. Payments already made are kept by the owner, including in a lease to own deal.
4. Links, printed material and search results that use the name stop reaching you.

The last point is the real cost. If you built your brand on a leased name, losing it means a forced rebrand. Our guide to [changing a domain name without losing SEO](/change-domain-name-without-losing-seo/) shows how much work a move involves, even when it is planned.

## Contract points to check before domain leasing

Treat a domain leasing contract the way you would treat a property lease. Read it in full, ask for changes in writing, and keep a signed copy.

### Domain leasing price and term

- The monthly or yearly fee, and whether it can rise on renewal.
- The total price in a lease to own deal, and whether any part of it is interest or a fee.
- The length of the term and how renewal works.

### Default and termination

- How long the grace period is after a missed payment, and how you are notified.
- Whether partial payments count, and whether you can catch up after a default.
- Whether any payments are returned if the deal ends early, by either side.
- What the owner may do with the name after termination.

### Control and ownership

- Who holds the name during the term, and in which registrar account.
- Your rights to set DNS, email and SSL records.
- When and how the name transfers to you after the final payment.
- Whether you can pay off the balance early.
- What happens if the owner dies, sells their business or stops responding.

### Trademarks and use

- Who is responsible if a third party claims the name infringes a trademark.
- Any limits on how you can use the name.

Read [trademarks and domain disputes](/trademarks-and-domain-disputes-udrp-basics/) before committing to any name, leased or bought. A dispute can take the name away regardless of the contract.

## When domain leasing makes sense

Domain leasing can be a reasonable choice in a few situations:

- You want to test a name with real customers before committing to buy it.
- You need a name for a fixed campaign or event that will end.
- The owner will not sell, but will rent, and the name is worth the risk.

It is a weaker choice when the name will carry your main brand for years. In that case every month you rent is a month you could lose it, and a fixed path to ownership is safer. The same applies if your email, invoices and customer accounts will all depend on the name.

## How to decide between leasing, a plan and buying outright

Answer these three questions before you choose:

1. **How long will you use the name?** For a short test, a plain lease can work. For your main brand, aim for ownership.
2. **What can you pay now?** If paying in full is not possible, compare a lease to own domain deal and a payment plan on total cost and default terms.
3. **Who controls the name if something goes wrong?** The answer should be clear in writing before you pay anything.
