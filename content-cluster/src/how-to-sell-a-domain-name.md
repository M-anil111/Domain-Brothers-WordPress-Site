If you have ever typed "how do I sell my domain" into a search bar, you have met plenty of conflicting advice. Selling well comes down to an honest valuation, a clear price, the right place to list, safe payment and a clean transfer. This guide is the seller's side of our guide on [how to buy a domain name for a website](/how-to-buy-a-domain-name-for-a-website/).

!takeaways In short
- Value the name before you set a price, using comparable sales and buyer fit, not hope.
- Pick a sales route that suits the name: a marketplace listing, a broker, or direct outreach.
- Get paid in full, through a method you trust, before you hand over the transfer code.
- Treat unsolicited "appraisal" emails and pushy buyers as warning signs, not opportunities.

## "Sell my domain": decide what you want from the sale first

!img:ib-sell-a|Sell my domain. Selling a domain name, step by step: Value, Price, List, Agree, Transfer|Selling a domain name, step by step.

Owners usually reach the "sell my domain" decision for one of three reasons. The business has rebranded, the project never launched, or someone has made an offer. Each reason sets a different deadline and a different floor price, so write yours down first.

Ask yourself two questions. How soon do you need the money? What is the lowest price you would accept without regret? A seller with no deadline can wait for the right buyer. A seller who needs cash this month should price for a faster sale and accept that it may be lower.

Also check that you can actually sell. Log in to your registrar, confirm the name is registered to you, and note the expiry date. A name that expires mid-negotiation is a problem you can avoid with a renewal.

## Step 1: Value the name before any "sell my domain" listing

Price is the step most sellers get wrong. Some ask for a figure based on what they paid, which tells a buyer nothing. Others ask for a figure based on a famous sale of a very different name. Neither helps you close a deal.

A useful valuation looks at the same factors buyers use:

- **Length and clarity.** Short, easy-to-spell names sell more easily.
- **Extension.** .com has the widest pool of buyers; country codes sell best inside their own country.
- **Meaning.** Real words and clear two-word phrases attract more end users than invented strings.
- **Comparable sales.** What similar names actually sold for, not what they were listed at.
- **Clean history.** No trademark conflict, no spam past, no penalty history.

Our guide on [how to value a premium domain name](/how-to-value-a-premium-domain-name/) explains the full method. If you want an outside view, ask a broker you trust to review the name and explain the reasoning before you list it.

## Step 2: Set a price, or decide to take offers

Owners who search "sell my domain" often expect one correct price to exist. In practice, you choose a pricing strategy that matches your name and your patience. There are three common choices.

| Pricing approach | Suits | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed "buy now" price | Names with clear comparables; sellers who want a fast, simple sale | Price too high and nothing happens; too low and you leave money behind |
| Minimum offer | Sellers who want to filter out low bids but stay flexible | Some buyers skip names without a visible price |
| Open to offers | Rare or unusual names with few comparable sales | Slower; more time spent on negotiation |

A visible price saves time for both sides. Buyers who can see a number can make a decision without a long email exchange. If you take offers, decide your floor in advance and do not reveal it.

### Should I sell my domain now or wait for a better offer?

Holding a name costs renewal fees every year, and demand for a given word can rise or fall. If the current offer sits inside your value range and the buyer looks genuine, accepting it is often the better decision. If the offer is far below your range, a polite counter-offer with a reason (comparable sales, length, extension) keeps the door open.

## Step 3: Where to sell domain names

The most common "sell my domain" question after price is where to list. There is no single best place to sell a domain name. The right route depends on the name's value, your timeline and how much work you want to do yourself.

### Marketplaces and listing services

Marketplaces let you list a name with a price or an offer form. Many also connect to a network of registrars, so your name shows up when buyers search for it. They usually charge a commission on the sale. A listing works best for names with an obvious buyer pool and a realistic price.

### Domain brokers

A broker markets the name to likely buyers, handles negotiation and keeps your identity private if you want. Brokers usually take a larger commission than marketplaces and often accept only higher-value names. They suit sellers with a strong name and little time.

### Direct outreach

You can contact businesses that would benefit from the name: companies using the same words on a different extension, or firms in the industry the name describes. Keep messages short, factual and polite. Never threaten, never imply the name could be used against their brand, and never target a trademark. That behaviour can lead to a dispute, as our guide to [trademarks and domain disputes](/trademarks-and-domain-disputes-udrp-basics/) explains.

### A "for sale" landing page

Pointing the domain at a simple page that says the name is for sale, with a contact form, catches buyers who type it in directly. It costs little and works alongside every other route.

The [Wikipedia overview of domain name resale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_name_resale) gives useful background on how the aftermarket developed and how these channels fit together.

## Step 4: Avoid the common seller scams

Owners of good names attract scammers, and a public "sell my domain" post on a forum or social network attracts even more. Most schemes follow a few patterns, and all of them are easy to spot once you know them.

- **The paid appraisal trap.** Someone offers to buy your name, but only after you get a valuation from a service they name. The service charges a fee, and the buyer disappears.
- **The fake escrow site.** The buyer insists on an escrow company you have never heard of. The site looks real and tells you payment is "secured". Nothing ever arrives.
- **The overpayment or reversed payment.** The buyer sends money by a method that can be reversed, asks you to transfer the domain at once, then cancels the payment.
- **The phishing login.** An email that looks like it is from your registrar asks you to "confirm the sale" by logging in through a link. It steals your account.

Use only payment methods and escrow services you have checked independently. Type your registrar's address into the browser yourself. If a buyer pushes hard for speed, slow down.

## Step 5: Get paid before you transfer

If you remember one rule from any "sell my domain" guide, make it this one: **payment clears before the domain moves**. Once a name leaves your account, getting it back is slow, uncertain and sometimes impossible.

There are a few safe ways to structure payment:

1. **Escrow.** A neutral company holds the buyer's money, you transfer the name, the buyer confirms, and the money is released to you. Read [domain escrow explained](/domain-escrow-explained/) before you choose one.
2. **A trusted marketplace's checkout.** Many marketplaces collect payment and manage the handover themselves.
3. **Bank transfer from a known buyer.** Wait until the funds have fully cleared in your account, not just "sent".

Put the agreed price, the domain name, who pays fees and the transfer deadline in writing, even if that is just a clear email both sides confirm.

## Step 6: Transfer the domain to the buyer

The transfer is the final step of the "sell my domain" process, and it is routine once payment is settled. The usual steps are:

1. Unlock the domain at your registrar.
2. Make sure your account email is current, because the registrar may send a confirmation.
3. Generate the transfer (EPP or auth) code and give it only to the confirmed buyer or the escrow service.
4. Approve the outgoing transfer if your registrar asks you to.
5. Confirm with the buyer that the name has arrived in their account.

Some registrars also allow a "push", where the name moves to another account at the same registrar without a code.

Check the rules in ICANN's [Transfer Policy](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/transfer-policy-2016-06-01-en): a change of registrant can trigger a 60-day lock on moving the name to a new registrar, unless the registrant opted out beforehand. Tell the buyer about it so it does not surprise them. Our guide to [domain transfers, EPP codes and DNS](/how-to-transfer-a-domain/) covers each step in detail.

## A quick "sell my domain" checklist

Before you list, run through this list:

- The name is in your registrar account and renewed well past the expected sale date.
- You have a written value range and a floor price.
- You have chosen at least one sales route and, ideally, a "for sale" page.
- You know which payment or escrow method you will accept.
- You will not release the transfer code until payment has cleared.
