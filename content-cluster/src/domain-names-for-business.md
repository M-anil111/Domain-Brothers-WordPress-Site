Domain names for business do more work than most founders expect: the name sits in every email address, invoice, ad and search result for as long as the company exists. A strong one is easy to say, spell and remember, and it fits the market you sell to. This guide explains how to judge candidates and protect the one you choose. It is part of our guide on [how to buy a domain name for a website](/how-to-buy-a-domain-name-for-a-website/), which covers the whole purchase from search to launch.

!takeaways In short
- Judge every candidate on the same tests: easy to say, easy to spell, short, relevant and free of trademark conflicts.
- Choose between a descriptive name, a brandable name and your exact company name based on how you plan to grow.
- Match the extension to where your customers are; .com remains the default for most international audiences.
- Secure the obvious misspellings and your country code, and keep the renewal details in a company account.

## Why domain names for business matter

!img:ib-business-a|Domain names for business. What a good business domain does: Easy to say, Easy to spell, Fits the brand, Right extension, Clear legally, Room to grow|What a good business domain does.


A business domain is the address customers type, the sender name on every email and the first thing many people see in a search result. When the name is clear, people find you without help. When it is awkward, they mistype it, send email to the wrong address or click a competitor whose name they remember better.

The cost of a weak name is quiet and ongoing. It shows up as lost referrals, extra ad spend to repeat the name, and support time spent spelling it out on the phone. That is why domain names for business deserve the same care as the company name itself, and why many companies choose the name and the domain together.

## What makes good domain names for business

Good domain names for business pass a few simple tests. Run every name on your shortlist through them before you look at prices.

- **The radio test.** Say the name aloud to someone who has never seen it. Can they type it correctly first time?
- **Length.** Fewer characters mean fewer typos and a cleaner logo, business card and email signature. Our guide to [short domain names](/short-domain-names/) explains why the shortest names are scarce and priced accordingly.
- **Relevance.** The name should fit what you sell now and what you plan to sell in five years. A name tied to one product or one city can become a limit later.
- **No hyphens or numbers** unless they are part of the brand. Both are hard to communicate by voice.
- **Distinct from competitors.** A name that differs by one letter from a rival invites confusion and, sometimes, legal trouble.

### Domain names for business that are easy to say and spell

Spelling is the test most often skipped. Words with several common spellings, doubled letters at a word join, and creative misspellings all lose visitors. If you have to say "with two Ls" every time you give the address, the name is costing you.

Ask five people to write the name after hearing it once. If two or more get it wrong, treat that as a warning, not a detail.

## How to choose a domain name: descriptive, brandable or company name

There are three broad styles of domain names for business, and each suits a different plan. Knowing how to choose a domain name mostly means choosing the style first, then the exact words.

| Style | Example form | Strengths | Trade-offs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | Two words that say what you do | Clear meaning, matches how people talk about the service | Harder to trademark, can limit expansion |
| Brandable | An invented or borrowed word | Distinct, easier to protect, room to grow | Needs marketing to give it meaning |
| Exact company name | Your registered business name | Consistent with legal name and signage | Only works if the name is short and clear |

Descriptive names work well for local services and focused online shops. Brandable names suit companies that expect to add products or enter new markets; our guide to [brandable domain names](/brandable-domain-names/) shows how to judge them. If your company name is long, a short brandable domain can serve as the trading name while the legal name stays on contracts.

## Choosing the extension for a company domain

The extension is part of the name people remember. For a company domain aimed at an international audience, .com is still the safest choice because many people type it by habit. If your customers are mostly in one country, the local country code can signal that you are based there and serve them directly.

Newer extensions can work for a specific audience, especially in technology. The risk is leakage: some customers will type .com anyway and reach whoever owns it. Our guide to [choosing a domain extension](/choosing-a-domain-extension-com-ca-io-and-more/) compares the main options in detail.

A practical rule: if you choose a non-.com extension, check who owns the .com version of the same name. If it belongs to a business in your field, pick a different name.

## Check trademarks and history before you commit

A name that conflicts with an existing trademark can cost you the domain and the brand built on it. Search the official trademark register in each market you sell to. In the United States, the [USPTO trademark search](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search) is free and covers registered and pending marks. Our guide to [trademarks and domain disputes](/trademarks-and-domain-disputes-udrp-basics/) explains how disputes over domain names for business usually arise.

Then check the domain's past. A name that once hosted spam or unrelated content may carry a poor reputation with email providers and search engines. Look at archived copies of the site, search for the name in quotes, and ask the seller what the name was used for.

## Protecting domain names for business after you buy

Once you own the main name, secure the gaps around it. Protecting domain names for business is cheap compared with recovering one later.

1. **Register the account in the company's name**, not an employee's personal account, and use a shared role email address for contact details.
2. **Turn on auto-renew** and keep a valid payment card on file. An expired business domain takes email and the website down at the same time.
3. **Enable the registrar lock and two-factor sign-in** to stop unauthorised transfers.
4. **Register common misspellings and your country code** if they are affordable, and redirect them to the main site.
5. **Write down who has access.** When staff leave, change passwords and remove their access the same day.

The Wikipedia article on the [domain names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_name) is a useful primer if your team needs background on how names, registrars and registries fit together.

## Common mistakes with domain names for business

The same few errors appear again and again when companies pick a name in a hurry. Most of them are cheap to avoid at the start and expensive to fix later.

- **Choosing a name only because it is free to register.** Availability is not a quality test. A cheap name that customers mistype costs more over time than a better name bought from its owner.
- **Following a trend.** Dropped vowels and odd suffixes can date quickly. A name should still sound sensible in ten years.
- **Tying the name to one product or one town.** If the business grows, the name can start to describe only part of it.
- **Ignoring email.** Say the full address aloud, as in name at your domain. If it sounds clumsy or invites spelling questions, customers will feel it on every message.
- **Leaving the account with a supplier.** Web designers and agencies sometimes register the name in their own account. Make sure the company is the registrant and holds the login.

When you review domain names for business with colleagues, show each candidate as it will appear in an email signature and on a phone screen. Context exposes problems that a list on a whiteboard hides.
