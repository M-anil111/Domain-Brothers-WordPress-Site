Learning how to buy a domain name from someone is the answer when the name you want is already registered and not listed for sale. The owner may be a business that stopped using it, an investor, or a person who registered it years ago and forgot about it. If you are wondering how to get a domain that is taken, this guide covers finding the owner, valuing the name, making contact, negotiating and closing the deal safely. It is part of our guide on [how to buy a domain name for a website](/how-to-buy-a-domain-name-for-a-website/).

!takeaways In short
- Look up the registration record first; privacy services often hide the owner but still pass messages on.
- Set your price range before you make contact, using comparable sales rather than hope.
- Keep the first message short and neutral, and do not reveal how much the name matters to you.
- Agree price, payment method and transfer steps in writing, and confirm the seller controls the name before you pay.

## Step 1: Find out who owns the name

!img:ib-someone-a|How to buy a domain name from someone. Buying a domain name from its owner: Look up, Research, Approach, Agree, Transfer|Buying a domain name from its owner.

The first step in how to buy a domain name from someone is finding out who that someone is. Every registered name has a registration record held by its registrar. The [ICANN Lookup tool](https://lookup.icann.org/en) queries that record through RDAP, the modern replacement for WHOIS, and shows the registrar, the creation date, the expiry date and the name servers.

Since data protection rules tightened, most records hide personal details. You will usually see the registrar's name and either a privacy service, a relay email address or a web form for contacting the registrant. That relay is the legitimate route to the owner, and messages sent through it do reach them.

### How to buy a domain name from someone when the owner is hidden

If the record shows only a privacy service, you still have options:

- **Use the relay.** Send your message through the registrar's contact form or the masked email address. Keep it short, because some relays strip formatting and links.
- **Check the website.** A live site or parked page often has a "this domain may be for sale" notice or a contact link.
- **Look for the owner elsewhere.** A business name in the site footer, an old archived copy of the site, or a company with a similar name can point you to the right person.
- **Ask a broker.** Brokers do this research every day and often know which investors hold which names.

Never try to trick a registrar or hosting company into revealing an owner's details. It does not work, and it can create legal problems.

## Step 2: Read the signals before you approach

What the name shows today tells you a lot about the owner and the likely price, and it shapes how to buy a domain name from someone in each case.

| What you see | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| A parked page with ads | Probably an investor; expect a market-based price |
| A "for sale" banner or price | The owner wants to sell; start with the listing terms |
| An active business website | The name is in use; a sale may be costly or impossible |
| No website, record years old | Possibly forgotten; the owner may be open to a fair offer |
| Expiry date within weeks | The owner may not renew; watch it before you approach |

If the name is close to expiry, our guide to [domain backorders and expired domains](/domain-backorder-and-expired-domains/) explains the typical deletion cycle. A renewal grace period follows expiry, then a redemption period of about 30 days and a pending delete period of about 5 days, though registrars vary.

## Step 3: Value the name before you make contact

In how to buy a domain name from someone, the buyer who has done the valuation first usually pays less. Decide what the name is worth before the owner names a figure. Once you hear a number, it anchors everything that follows.

Score the name on length, extension, meaning, brandability and clean history, then look at what similar names actually sold for. Our guide on [how to value a premium domain name](/how-to-value-a-premium-domain-name/) walks through a repeatable method. Then work out what the name is worth to your business in customers gained and marketing saved.

Write down two numbers: the price you would be glad to pay and the price at which you walk away. Knowing how to buy a domain name from someone is largely knowing when to stop.

## Step 4: Approach the owner and negotiate

When you contact domain owner addresses or relay forms, your first message sets the tone. Keep it brief, polite and neutral; in how to buy a domain name from someone, a calm opening gets more replies than an eager one. Say you are interested in buying the name, ask whether the owner would consider selling, and ask what price they have in mind.

Avoid these mistakes in the first message:

- **Revealing your company or project.** A well-funded buyer's name can multiply the asking price.
- **Making the first offer too high.** You cannot go lower once you have named a figure.
- **Writing at length.** A long message signals urgency and gives the owner more to work with.
- **Threatening legal action** when you have no trademark claim. It ends conversations and rarely succeeds.

If the owner does not reply within a week or two, send one short follow-up. Silence after that usually means no, or that the message never arrived.

### Negotiate the price

Negotiation is the part of how to buy a domain name from someone that tests your patience. Most domain negotiations take a few rounds. Expect the owner's first figure to be high and leave room to move from your first offer.

Back each counter-offer with a reason: comparable sales, the name's length or extension, or the fact that you have alternatives. Move in smaller steps as you approach your limit, so the owner sees the gap closing. If the gap stays wide, say politely that you will look at other names, and mean it. Owners sometimes return weeks later with a lower figure.

When you agree, write the terms down: the exact name, the price, the payment method, who pays any fees, who starts the transfer, and the deadline.

## Paying and transferring: how to buy a domain name from someone safely

The last stage of how to buy a domain name from someone is the one where mistakes cost the most. Money and the domain should move in a fixed order that protects both sides. Before paying, confirm that the seller controls the name: ask them to change a DNS record or post a short code on the site, or to send a message from the registrant email address.

In a private sale between strangers, an escrow company can hold the payment until the name arrives in your account. Our guide to [domain escrow](/domain-escrow-explained/) explains how that works and what it costs. Never pay by wire, crypto or gift card to someone you cannot verify.

The transfer itself happens in one of two ways: a push to your account at the same registrar, or a transfer (EPP) code that lets you move the name to your own registrar. Under ICANN's [Transfer Policy](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/transfer-policy-2016-06-01-en), a change of registrant can typically trigger a 60-day lock on moving to another registrar, unless the owner opts out where the registrar allows it. Our guide to [transfers, EPP codes and DNS](/how-to-transfer-a-domain/) covers each step. Once the name is in your account, change the contact details and turn on auto-renew.

## A checklist for how to buy a domain name from someone

Run through this list before you send any money. Each item takes minutes and removes a common way for a private sale to go wrong.

1. **Spelling and extension.** Confirm the exact name in writing, letter by letter, including the extension.
2. **Control.** The seller has proved control of the name, for example by changing a DNS record you specify.
3. **Trademarks.** You have searched the trademark register in your main markets and found no conflict.
4. **History.** You have checked archived copies of the site and searched the name for spam or misuse.
5. **Written terms.** Price, payment method, fees, transfer method and deadline are agreed in one written message both sides have confirmed.
6. **Payment route.** You are paying through a traceable method or a neutral third party, never a method that cannot be reversed.
7. **Your registrar account.** It is ready, in your company's name, with two-factor sign-in enabled, so the name can arrive without delay.

If any item is missing, pause. A genuine seller will wait a day while you check; pressure to skip a step is itself a warning sign.
